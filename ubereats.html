01 — The Problem

The app knows everything.
And does nothing with it.

Uber Eats collects an enormous amount of data about every user — past orders, favourite restaurants, dietary filters, time of day patterns, location. And then, every single session, it greets you like a stranger. The home screen shows you what's popular near you — not what you actually eat.

For late-night workers especially, this is a failure. They order at irregular hours, rely on a rotation of comfort foods, and have no time to browse. The app should already know what they want. It has the data. It just doesn't use it.

"The app treats every session like a first visit. But for someone ordering at 2am after a 12-hour shift, this isn't discovery — it's friction."

1
Reorder is buried
Past orders live in a separate tab. No prediction on the home screen. No "order your usual?" moment. Every session starts from scratch.
2
Dietary filters reset
Set "vegetarian" in a session — it's forgotten next time. The app doesn't carry preferences forward. You re-filter every order.
3
No time-awareness
2am on a Tuesday is a different need than Saturday lunch. The app shows the same recommendations regardless of when, why, or how often you order.
4
Shallow personalisation
"Recommended for you" means popular items nearby — not items that match your actual order history. It's proximity, not personalisation.
02 — User Persona

Designing for
the late-night shift.

Same approach as every project — every design decision is made for one specific person. Meet Dara. She works unpredictable hours, orders comfort food at irregular times, and has zero patience for browsing when she's exhausted.

🏥

"It's 1:30am. I just finished a 12-hour shift. I know I want Thai food or that ramen place. I don't want to scroll through 200 restaurants. I want the app to know me by now."
Goals
Order in under 30 seconds — she's too tired to browse at 1am
Vegetarian filter always on — never wants to check manually
App predicts her rotation — Thai Tuesday, ramen after night shifts
Pay and forget — Apple Pay, no fuss, order arrives, done
Frustrations
Starts from scratch every time — no memory of what she ordered last
Vegetarian filter disappears — meat dishes appear after every new session
2am recommendations are irrelevant — shows lunch spots that are closed
Has to rebuild her usual order — same ramen bowl, same customisations, every time
03 — The AI Feature

Three capabilities.
One seamless experience.

The feature isn't a single button — it's three AI capabilities working together. Smart Reorder surfaces her usual orders. Dietary Memory keeps her preferences permanently. AI Prediction shows her what she'll want before she asks. Together they make the app feel like it actually knows her.

01
Smart Reorder
Her last 3 orders surface on the home screen — ordered by how recently and how often she's ordered them. One tap to reorder any of them.
02
Dietary Memory
Preferences set once, stored permanently. Vegetarian filter, allergen flags, spice level — all applied automatically to every recommendation, every session.
03
AI Prediction
The app learns her patterns — Thai on Tuesday evenings, ramen after night shifts. It shows the predicted order at the top before she even types anything.
→
One tap to confirm
Customisations saved from last order. Apple Pay default. Face ID confirms. Under 10 seconds from open to ordered.

Why Smart Reorder works here
The AI card appears at the top — not buried in a tab. It shows her two most likely orders based on time (1:34am) and pattern (ramen after night shifts). One tap reorders without rebuilding anything.
Vegetarian filter — already applied
Both suggested orders are vegetarian. The "Vegetarian" category pill is pre-selected. She never had to set it this session — Dietary Memory applied it automatically.
Design principle
Show the most likely action first. The AI card is the first tappable element on screen — not a banner, not a promotion. It's the main action.

05 — What I Learned

AI features fail
when they feel like AI.

1
The best AI is invisible.
The feature never says "our machine learning model analysed your behaviour patterns." It says "your usual?" The technology disappears into the outcome. If the user notices the AI, it's probably in the way — not helping.
2
Transparency builds trust — show the why.
Showing "ordered 5x after night shifts" next to the prediction changes how the feature feels entirely. Without it, an AI recommendation feels like a guess. With it, it feels like the app actually knows you. The "why" is part of the design.
3
Speculative design requires real constraints.
This is a concept — the AI system doesn't exist yet. But every capability shown is grounded in data Uber Eats already collects: order history, time stamps, location, dietary filters. The concept is speculative in form, not in possibility. That distinction matters.
